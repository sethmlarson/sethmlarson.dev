# Gaming’s all-digital future doesn’t include “Sonic the Hedgehog 3” and “Pikmin 2”

Sony announced that all games from 2028 and onwards
would be digital-only. It seems likely that Xbox will follow and that Nintendo will continue
making “physical” releases for first-party games during
the Switch 2 generation.

In the all-digital gaming future, every game platform
will be subject to the same problems and constraints as media streaming services
like Netflix and Spotify.
We can see hints of how this will affect future games and creative freedom in the medium
by looking at re-releases of older games, in particular: Sonic the Hedgehog 3 and Pikmin 2.

<!-- more -->

<hr>

The core problem is that digital games are licenses, which forces
an ongoing relationship between you and the owner of the media.
And because there’s an ongoing relationship with you, any
ongoing licensing relationships that the media owner has
*also affect you and your games*. If a license for any media that
is part of the game expires or is affected, then the developer
must take action to remediate the “issue” in the game.

This is already commonplace for games that feature music,
such as Rock Band or Guitar Hero, but maybe players find this
more “expected” because these games are essentially
a music streaming service in themselves.
But what about games that aren’t like this on the surface?

[Sonic the Hedgehog 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonic_the_Hedgehog_3) was originally published in 1994, and
beyond the game being great to play, the soundtrack is
really something special. I have fond memories of dancing
to the [end credits music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G8bNZboAPfc) on my parents’ bed with my brother as a kid after beating the game.

Unfortunately, this track and many others in the game
would not survive our all-digital future. [Michael Jackson’s
alleged uncredited involvement with Sonic 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonic_the_Hedgehog_3#Michael_Jackson's_involvement)
has meant that all re-releases of Sonic 3 since 2009
have not included the entire original soundtrack. If this
game were released only digitally today, it can be assumed that
these tracks would be pulled from the game, just as
is done for Rock Band and Guitar Hero when licensing terms
expire or there are legal issues.

[Pikmin 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikmin_2) was published in 2004,
and the main objective is collecting “treasure” from an alien planet in the form
of food, toys, and, most surprisingly, [items featuring real-life brands](https://sethmlarson.dev/pikmin-2-international-treasure-hoard)
like Duracell, <nobr>Dr. Pepper</nobr>, <nobr>Yoo-Hoo</nobr>, and Snapple. This artistic
decision and the work to license and include these brands were a substantial part of the surprise and delight
while playing the game.

However, you can see that these licensing terms
eventually run out, too: in all re-releases of Pikmin 2 from 2023 onwards,
the real-life brands are [replaced with generic fictional brands](https://www.pikminwiki.com/Pikmin_2_(Nintendo_Switch)#List_of_treasures_with_different_appearances).
It’s not clear to me whether licensing agreements with
so many brands would be undertaken in the future if work would
have to be done to create fictional models and textures anyway
once the license terms expire.

If it wasn’t clear from this post, I’m not excited for an all-digital
future of games. An all-digital future for games means the medium will be further constrained
creatively, in addition to the negative impacts on ownership and preservation.
Read-only physical media does more for gaming
than being something to collect and own: developers can make
choices that they won’t have to walk back in 20 years,
and players can continue to enjoy games *as the developers
intended* long after licensing terms expire.
