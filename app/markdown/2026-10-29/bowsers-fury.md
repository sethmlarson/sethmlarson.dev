# Bowser’s Fury outshines Sunshine, SA2B, and Billy Hatcher

[Bowser’s Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowser%27s_Fury) masters the “*multiple goals; same geometry*”
and “*expressiveness through jank*”
themes from GameCube-era platforming adventure games that I consider some of my
favorites:
[Super Mario Sunshine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Sunshine),
[Sonic Adventure 2: Battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonic_Adventure_2),
and [Billy Hatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Hatcher_and_the_Giant_Egg). If these games mean anything to
you, then *do not miss Bowser’s Fury*.
The entire game is one cohesive, snackable,
free-flowing experience with a complete break
from Super Mario’s arcade and hardware-constrained
past, removing mainstays like <nobr>1-UPs</nobr>, <nobr>game-overs</nobr>,
segmented levels, and linear progression.

## Perfecting “Multiple goals; Same geometry”

If you’re a game designer and your game has *hundreds of goals
for the player to accomplish*, it’s better to re-use the
beautifully hand-crafted levels than to create unique (and time-consuming...)
geometry for each goal ring, shine, or emblem. But there
was one glaring issue with each of the three games
I’m referencing today that becomes obvious
after only a few minutes of playing Bowser’s Fury...

*“Oh wow, there’s no menus!”* I had my brother try Bowser’s Fury shortly
after wrapping the game myself. After he collected his first Cat Shine, the gameplay
just... *kept going*. The difference is night and day compared to games from
our childhood.
Long-jumping from Scamper Shores’ lighthouse into the lake rewards you
with a music change and a new mission: Pounce Bounce Isle. The gameplay loop feels
this [seamless](https://sethmlarson.dev/mario-kart-world-and-seamless-media) until you’re at the very end, cleaning up the last few Cat Shines.

Paging through menus to select a level and being kicked out mid-adventure now
seems clunky in comparison. I distinctly remember paging through guide books
to recall which Shine Sprite, Emblem, or Mission I had to select in
Super Mario Sunshine, Billy Hatcher, and Sonic Adventure 2, respectively, to
find what I was after. And if you found a secret goal while you were on your
adventure, it almost felt like being *punished*, because you’d be forced
to replay the level after getting kicked back to the “overworld”.

I could feel the playfulness and constraint from the level designers
in Bowser’s Fury, too. It felt as though the designers created the original geometry
with a singular goal, and then, *without allowing themselves to change
that geometry at all*, had to add five times more goals.
You’d find yourself traversing levels backwards, exploring previously
inaccessible areas, or being surprised that the world was much bigger
than you previously thought. In Bowser’s Fury the “key levels” were used
for this, forcing players to take different paths with a constrained move-set
while carrying the key to a locked cage. Slipskate Slope’s key level reminded
me of Billy Hatcher’s “Climb up the hill” level in Giant Palace, where in both the goal is to
ascend the slope that you just slid or rolled down in the prior level.

You can tell that this was no small feat, either. Every
time I loaded the game at the start of a play session, the
initial loading time would be substantial (~30 seconds?).
That’s because the entire world needs to be mostly loaded
at all times; there’s only one level, after all! I found myself
selecting the load button and then arranging the pillows on the couch or grabbing
a Diet Coke instead of waiting for Mario to appear.

## “Jank” (aka “Expressiveness”)

I was doing some research into Sonic Adventure’s design to figure out why Bowser’s Fury
reminded me so much of playing the Sonic Adventure games.
One review [highlighted an under-appreciated aspect](https://youtu.be/Mo4M31mhWXY?si=uTcWxPTKshuc9rv9&t=316)
of Sonic Adventure’s design: the idea that “jank” is evidence of “expressiveness”
in the player’s movement and choices.

Somewhat famously, in Sonic Adventure you’d sometimes fall through the floor,
or maybe a jump or rail-grind *wouldn’t quite connect*,
causing you to awkwardly fly off the stage to your doom.
This can be off-putting, but what it means for the player is that *your movement
is your own*. *Platforming is never a set piece*; there
aren’t any tracks or invisible walls keeping you on the geometry.

Bowser’s Fury feels that way, but with Nintendo’s absurd amount of
polish that keeps you immersed and in the moment. “Oh you can stand on *THAT*?”
was a common refrain while searching for a route
in key levels. Fury Bowser instantly changes any careful moment into a hurried
frenzy as you dodge his attacks from all angles. The Plessie Medals have you crashing
through trees and enemies, desperately
squeezing a high-speed dinosaur through geometry designed for Mario’s footprint.

Through all the chaos and impromptu situations, you won’t find yourself asking
whether what you’re doing is intentional. You melt into the controller and the moment.
